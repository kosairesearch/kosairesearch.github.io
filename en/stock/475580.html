Fast Revenue Growth, Profitability Still a Test

Alux is expanding rapidly into defense and industrial drones with fast-growing sales, but expansion costs and one-off items mean operating profit has yet to return to breakeven.

02

Business structure

Founded in 2015, Alux is an ed-tech company built on drone and robot manufacturing, supplying self-made educational robots and drones to elementary after-school programs and expanding into related educational content and services.

According to a media report, first-half 2024 revenue was split among robots (15%), drones (22%), other teaching materials (8%), government-commissioned services (10%), education-operation services (31%), and the academy business (13%).

Citing company data, one report noted that the revenue share of the drone manufacturing new-growth business rose roughly threefold, from about 6.7% in 2023 to about 19.8% in 2024.

The core competitive edge in drones lies in parts localization: Alux is said to be the only domestic company that independently develops and produces the flight controller (FC), a key drone component.

As a result, the company is viewed as having secured cost competitiveness through in-house production of core parts such as the FC and a mass-production system, giving it differentiated competitiveness in customized drone markets.

In the education robot segment, it holds the number-one domestic market share in education robots (about 35%) with a lineup of more than 70 products spanning toddlers to adults.

More recently, through an acquisition in the defense drone space and commercialization of autonomous flight solutions, the company has been broadening its focus toward industrial and defense applications; a July supply contract with Hanwha Aerospace for drone mission-equipment-compatible payload modules, an Army contract for education-purpose commercial drones, supply of physical-AI autonomous flight drones to large logistics, heavy-industry and automotive customers, and delivery of special-purpose drones to local governments all contributed to performance growth.

Competitively, Alux competes with domestic peers such as RoboRobo in education robots, while in drones it seeks differentiation through localization and export capability against Chinese players such as DJI that dominate the global market.

04

Earnings analysis

Consolidated revenue was flat at about KRW 54.8 billion in 2023 and KRW 55.0 billion in 2024 before jumping to KRW 63.0 billion in 2025, but operating profit swung from KRW 5.03 billion (9.2% margin) in 2023 and KRW 3.00 billion (5.5%) in 2024 to an operating loss of KRW -10.47 billion (-16.6%) in 2025.

Net income attributable to owners likewise flipped from KRW 3.96 billion in 2023 and KRW 2.82 billion in 2024 to a loss of KRW -9.39 billion in 2025, while the debt-to-equity ratio rose sharply from 10.5% in 2024 to 105.2% in 2025 and operating cash flow turned negative at KRW -0.16 billion.

On a quarterly basis, revenue of KRW 12.73 billion and an operating loss of KRW 3.86 billion in the second quarter of 2025 improved to revenue of KRW 18.71 billion and a narrower operating loss of KRW 0.67 billion in the third quarter, with a brief net profit of KRW 0.60 billion, before the fourth quarter saw revenue of KRW 20.40 billion accompanied by a wider operating loss of KRW 4.26 billion and a net loss of KRW 4.79 billion.

Moving into 2026, revenue was KRW 15.24 billion with an operating loss of KRW 2.51 billion in the first quarter, and KRW 21.91 billion in revenue with an operating loss of KRW 1.26 billion and a near-breakeven net loss of KRW 0.03 billion in the second quarter, showing revenue scaling up alongside a somewhat narrower operating loss.

Over the trailing four quarters (third quarter of 2025 through second quarter of 2026), net income attributable to owners totaled about KRW -7.45 billion, indicating that despite quarter-to-quarter swings, the company remains in a net-loss position on an annual basis.

This pattern reflects costs tied to expansion into defense and industrial drones, M&A, and capacity additions running ahead of the pace of revenue growth.

05

Industry analysis

The global drone market has long been dominated by Chinese manufacturers, but the landscape is shifting. According to market commentary, DJI's discontinuation of education-drone sales and tightened US restrictions on Chinese-made drones have reshaped the market for lightweight drones, expanding export opportunities.

In Korea, the government is pushing drone localization and market expansion; notably, tied to the '500,000 Drone Warriors' training policy, the Ministry of National Defense plans to introduce about 60,000 drones over four years, starting with 11,137 this year and rising to 15,368 in 2027, 16,898 in 2028 and 18,189 in 2029.

Government-led defense drone projects can also slip in timing, however; one report noted that the coastal reconnaissance drone program had been halted over a 'made-in-China' controversy and has since restarted, but its completion is now projected to be three years later than originally planned.

In the education robot market, Korea's declining student population from low birthrates is a structural headwind, partially offset by policy-driven demand such as mandatory AI and coding education.

Competitively, Alux is cited as one of the few companies with in-house part design and mass-production capability, giving it a relatively secured revenue base while many domestic and overseas peers remain unprofitable.

Overall, the industry sits at a juncture where structural growth opportunities from de-China supply chain shifts and rising defense demand coexist with uncertainty from policy-project delays and intensifying competition.

06

Outlook

The company has set an ambitious target based on the orders it has secured and its expanded production capacity.

A recent report noted that based on orders secured this year and expanded mass production, the company aims for revenue growth of nearly double the prior year, entry into the KRW 100 billion annual revenue range, a swing to profit, and improved profitability.

Compared with the confirmed 2025 results of KRW 63.0 billion in revenue and an operating loss of KRW 10.47 billion, this represents a fairly ambitious goal whose achievement will need to be verified through upcoming quarterly results.

In the industrial and defense segment, autonomous flight technology has reached the commercialization stage and is being actually deployed for inspection and patrol at logistics and heavy-industry sites and for unmanned nighttime security at automotive plants.

On the defense side, the company is developing key components such as cameras, gimbals and data links in parallel, expanding its foundation to meet demand for non-Chinese drone supply chains, and holding discussions with overseas defense companies on joint product development and production.

Government-ordered defense projects such as the coastal reconnaissance drone program appear to be progressing through procedures including operational testing, though the timing of final contractor selection remains uncertain.

The company has also said it is pursuing joint ventures, proof-of-concept projects in multiple countries, and local operation and maintenance centers for its overseas business, leaving domestic defense contract wins and overseas expansion as key variables for future results.

08

Bull factors

Expansion into Defense and Industrial Drones

New revenue sources have been secured in succession, including the supply of payload modules to Hanwha Aerospace, the winning of an Army educational commercial drone project, and the supply of autonomous flight drones to major logistics, heavy industry, and automotive corporations.

This can be seen as a factor diversifying the revenue structure that had previously been concentrated in educational robots and drones.

Following the acquisition of a defense drone specialist company, the possibility of expanded domestic defense orders has been discussed, and related infrastructure and test evaluation procedures are also underway.

Potential Benefit from De-China Supply Chain Shift

A trend of increasing demand for non-Chinese drones is being observed, driven by DJI's discontinuation of educational drone sales and the strengthening of U.S. regulations on Chinese-made drones.

AILUX is regarded as one of the few domestic companies that independently designs and produces core components such as flight controllers (FC), electronic speed controllers (ESC), and motors, putting it in a relatively favorable position amid this trend.

A Shinhan Investment Corp analyst stated in an analysis reported in March 2026, "As drones have become weaponized, the U.S. government has begun sanctioning Chinese companies that account for 85% of the U.S. market."

Policy-Driven Demand Expansion

Government-led policies, such as the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport's designation as a leading supply chain stabilization enterprise and the Ministry of National Defense's drone force buildup plan, are underpinning demand across the drone industry as a whole.

In the education sector, national education projects such as the introduction of digital textbooks and the Neulbom School program continue to serve as a stable revenue base. This policy-driven demand is characterized by relatively lower volatility compared to private-sector demand.

09

Bear factors

Profitability Recovery Not Yet Confirmed

Annual operating profit for 2025 turned negative at -10.47 billion won, and operating losses continued in the first and second quarters of 2026 at -2.51 billion won and -1.26 billion won, respectively.

In terms of net income, the company temporarily posted a profit in the third quarter of 2025, but returned to a substantial net loss in the fourth quarter, making it difficult to confirm a stable turnaround to profitability. A characteristic of this period is that the rate of cost increase outpaced the rate of revenue growth.

Balance Sheet Shift and Cash Flow Pressure

The debt-to-equity ratio rose sharply from 10.5% in 2024 to 105.2% in 2025, and operating cash flow also turned negative at -160 million won in 2025.

This represents a departure from the previously maintained low debt-to-equity financial structure, suggesting that fundraising and cash management have emerged as key management points during this expansion phase.

Risk of Delays in Government-Ordered Defense Projects

The coastal reconnaissance UAV project was suspended due to controversy over Chinese-made components before being resumed, and it has been reported that the completion date will be delayed by about three years compared to the original plan.

Schedule volatility in government-led defense projects can lead to uncertainty in the timing of related revenue recognition.

Risk factors

Policy and Regulatory Risk

Performance is significantly exposed to policy variables such as drone localization requirements, defense budget allocation, and the direction of U.S. regulations on Chinese drones. If policy stances change or budgets are delayed, the timing of related order intake and revenue recognition could be affected accordingly.

The Ministry of National Defense's multi-year drone procurement plan must also go through annual budget deliberations each year, making it difficult to treat it as a confirmed figure.

Financial and Profitability Risk

Since 2025, continued operating losses and net losses, along with a sharp rise in the debt-to-equity ratio, have increased the burden of financial soundness management.

The company's stated targets of 100 billion won in annual revenue and a turnaround to profitability are not confirmed results but company-set goals, so whether they are achieved will need to be verified through future disclosures.

Competitive and Execution Risk

The educational robot market faces a structural constraint in the form of declining student numbers due to low birth rates, and the industrial and defense drone market has numerous competitors, including existing major defense and aerospace companies.

Execution risk may arise in the process of organizational and technological integration with the defense drone subsidiary acquired through the M&A.

Overall view

Alux is building a new growth pillar in defense and industrial autonomous drones on top of its existing education robot and drone business, and first-half 2026 revenue rose sharply year on year to mark the largest half-year revenue since the company's founding.

However, after operating profit swung to a loss in full-year 2025, operating losses have continued through the first half of 2026, and a sharp rise in the debt ratio points to a shift in the balance sheet, meaning a profitability recovery has not yet been definitively confirmed.

New revenue streams in defense and industrial applications—such as the Hanwha Aerospace supply contract and the Army's education-purpose commercial drone order—along with potential benefits from tightened US restrictions on Chinese-made drones, stand out as positive factors.

On the other hand, the history of delays in government-ordered defense projects, the slow return to breakeven, and the shift in financial structure are variables that warrant continued attention.

The company has set targets of entering the KRW 100 billion revenue range and returning to profit, but it should be noted that these are company-set goals rather than confirmed results.

Upcoming quarterly results and the progress of defense projects will likely be the key evidence for judging whether this company's growth-and-profitability story actually materializes.
